## 10 Concepts
These are not in any particular order.

## 1. Seed
- Input: closed fist = plant a seed at that position; raising the fist upward = stem grows taller with arm height; opening the palm = bloom unfurls into petals. 
- Output: a growing plant rendered live on screen. 
- What changes: seed → stem → bloom, a full life-stage progression tied to hand shape and height. 
- How the viewer knows: the body metaphors are already intuitive (cupping a fist reads as "holding something small," raising an arm reads as "growing," opening a palm reads as "releasing") — each stage's visual payoff confirms the guess.

## 2. Weather System
- Input: standing calm/still = clear sky; fast, erratic movement = storm clouds gather and darken; raising both arms = clouds part and sun breaks through. 
- Output: a generative sky scene. 
- What changes: the whole visual mood swings between clear, stormy, and sunlit states based on how you're moving. 
- How the viewer knows: weather-as-mood is a universal metaphor, and the shift is big and immediate enough to notice by accident.

## 3. Molt
- Input: standing still for several seconds = a shell slowly builds up around your silhouette; a sudden outward "burst" gesture (arms flung open) = the shell cracks and falls away, revealing a new color/texture underneath. 
- Output: a shell layer that forms and shatters. 
- What changes: buildup → crack → reveal is a full loop that can repeat. 
- How the viewer knows: the slow crust forming while still is visible on its own, and flinging your arms open to "break out" is a gesture people try instinctively once they notice the shell.

## 4. Tug of War
- Input: two hands (or two people) each anchor one end of a line rendered on screen; whichever hand extends further/pulls harder moves a glowing orb toward their side. 
- Output: a rope-pull visualization with a win state — full displacement triggers a color burst. 
- What changes: orb position, and a burst when one side fully wins. 
- How the viewer knows: tug of war is an already-known game, so people test it instantly — and it naturally invites two strangers to play against each other.

## 5. Firefly Jar
- Input: silence/stillness = a single dim, flickering point of light; speaking or making sound = it brightens and multiplies, more particles the longer you sustain sound; sustained loud sound = the swarm briefly organizes into a shape. 
- Output: a particle swarm reacting to voice. 
- What changes: population and brightness build with vocal engagement, climaxing in a shape. 
- How the viewer knows: coaxing a shy creature to life by talking to it is an intuitive thing to try, and the payoff (multiplying lights) rewards it immediately.

## 6. The Held Gaze
- Input: your face detected and held still/centered = an eye on screen slowly opens and grows more detailed the longer you hold eye contact; looking away = it fades shut. 
- Output: an eye that "wakes up" with sustained attention.
- What changes: how open/detailed the eye becomes, tied to a staring contest with the piece itself. 
- How the viewer knows: people instinctively test whether a screen is "watching them back," and the slow reveal rewards patience.

## 7. Ink in Water
- Input: a sharp flick or push gesture toward the camera injects a burst of colored "ink" into a fluid simulation at that screen position; the ink diffuses and swirls on its own afterward; too many gestures too fast muddy the colors into a single dark blob. 
- Output: fluid-sim ink diffusion driven by discrete gesture injections. 
- What changes: color and swirl pattern build with each gesture, with an "overload" state if overdone. 
- How the viewer knows: flicking a hand to "throw" color is an almost painterly, obvious thing to try, and watching the ink drift afterward rewards stepping back.

## 8. Exposure
- Input: over the full unattended runtime, the piece builds a long-exposure composite of everyone who's passed through; standing still for several seconds "burns in" a clear ghost of you into that composite, while walking through quickly leaves only a faint streak. 
- Output: an accumulating residue layer beneath the live video. 
- What changes: how strongly your image gets etched into the piece's persistent "memory." 
- How the viewer knows: they can watch a faint copy of themselves darken in real time the longer they hold still, and see evidence (other people's faint ghosts) that others did the same.

## 9. Constellation
- Input: each person who enters frame gets a point of light that traces their movement; multiple people generate multiple points, with lines connecting ones that are near each other; leaving fades your point out slowly rather than instantly. 
- Output: a live constellation map of everyone currently or recently present. 
- What changes: number of points, connecting lines, and afterglow, scaling with how many people are around. 
- How the viewer knows: seeing a point of light track their own movement 1:1 is immediately legible, and noticing it connect to someone else's invites moving toward or away from them to test it.

## 10. Sketch
- Input: your index fingertip position and movement speed (you've already got fingertip tracking built from earlier, so this reuses that). 
- Output: a single continuous fine ink line trails from your fingertip across the frame, rendered like a brush stroke on a cream/bone background — fashion-illustration style, not a cursor or a glowing trail. 
- What changes: line weight responds to how fast you move — slow, deliberate motion lays down a thicker, more confident stroke, while fast motion thins the line out, almost
- How the viwer knows: dragging a fingertip and watching a line appear behind it is about as intuitive as interaction gets — people reach out to "draw" almost reflexively, and the line's immediate 1:1 follow confirms it instantly.
